Every unit still in use

342 units people actually write down today, across 30 domains, each with where it came from and who decided it. Not a museum of cubits and hogsheads.

The five strands

Almost every unit in the catalogue traces back to one of five things. The strands are not tidy, and a unit can pass through several definitions over its life, but the root of each one is usually clear. The counts are how the 342 units divide.

How definitions got fixed

Separately from where a unit came from is the question of what pins it down. That has moved through three phases, and the third finished only recently.

antiquity – 1799

Local consensus

A unit was whatever the local authority said it was, often embodied in a bar or a vessel kept at a church or guild hall. The same word meant different amounts a hundred miles away. The English foot and the Paris foot were not the same foot.

1799 – 2019

One artifact for everyone

The French Revolution produced the mètre des Archives and the kilogramme des Archives: single physical objects, replicated and distributed. It solved the consistency problem and created a new one. The international prototype kilogram drifted about 50 µg against its own copies over a century, and nobody could say which had changed.

20 May 2019 –

Constants only

The SI now fixes seven constants by decree (ΔνCs, c, h, e, k, NA, Kcd) and derives all seven base units from them. No object defines anything. Any sufficiently equipped lab can realize a kilogram from scratch with a Kibble balance, and the last artifact standard was retired.

Where this stands in 2026

In June 2026 the BIPM published version 4.01 of the SI Brochure, which for the first time states plainly that non-SI units hold no special status within the SI; the old “accepted for use” table became a wider catalogue of conversion factors.

The 28th CGPM meets 13–15 October 2026 in Versailles with three time resolutions on the table: a future definition of the second based on optical clocks, the technical steps to keep UTC continuous, and a first international lunar reference time scale. The second is the unit most likely to be redefined next.

On the word “unit”

A fair number of entries here are not units in the strict sense. Mohs hardness, the Beaufort scale, the enhanced Fujita scale and the Apgar score are ordinal scales: 8 is harder than 4, but not twice as hard, and the intervals are not equal. Percent, Mach and Reynolds number are dimensionless ratios wearing a name. Both kinds are marked in the catalogue, because they are what people mean when they say “unit” in the field, and pretending otherwise makes a reference less useful.

Values marked exactly are definitional, not measured. Everything else is rounded. Where a unit has competing definitions (the calorie, the horsepower, the gallon, the ton) all the live ones are listed, because the ambiguity is the point.

The units of the day are ten drawn at random and redrawn daily, not a ranking.
