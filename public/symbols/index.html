Where each symbol came from, who chose it, what it displaced, and the other three things it means in somebody else's field.

Five ways a symbol gets its shape

Mathematical notation was not designed. It accumulated, one printer and one textbook at a time, and almost every glyph on this page was chosen by an identifiable person for a reason they usually did not bother to write down. Where the reason survives, it falls into one of five patterns.

Why everything collides

The Latin alphabet has 26 letters, the Greek 24, and physics alone has several thousand named quantities. Collision is not sloppiness; it is arithmetic. The working solution has always been context: σ is a standard deviation to a statistician, a stress to an engineer, a conductivity to an electrical engineer, a surface tension to a chemist, the Stefan-Boltzmann constant to an astronomer and a Pauli matrix to a physicist, and no one in any of those rooms is confused.

The cost lands on people who move between fields, and on anyone reading a paper from a neighbouring discipline. That is what the also means line on each entry is for. It is the field this reference exists to provide, and the reason a plain symbol table is not much use.

Two collisions are worth knowing because they cause real errors rather than momentary confusion. θ and φ are swapped between the mathematics and physics conventions for spherical coordinates, so a formula copied across the boundary silently computes the wrong thing. And {A,B} is an anticommutator in quantum mechanics and a Poisson bracket in classical mechanics, which are related but not the same object, in a field where people routinely write both.

On what is and is not recorded

A surprising amount of this history is simply lost. Nobody knows why Clausius chose S for entropy; the story that it honours Sadi Carnot is conjecture with no supporting evidence. Nobody recorded why Sommerfeld chose α for the fine-structure constant, or why Schrödinger chose ψ for the wavefunction, or who first put a circumflex on an operator. Where that is the case, the entry says so rather than repeating a plausible-sounding invention.

Several popular etymologies are folklore and are labelled as such: that ∅ is a Greek phi (André Weil said it is the Norwegian Ø), that ∞ depicts a Möbius band (which postdates it by two centuries), that × is the cross of St Andrew, and that x for the unknown comes from Arabic shay by way of Spanish. Descartes simply worked backwards from the end of the alphabet.
